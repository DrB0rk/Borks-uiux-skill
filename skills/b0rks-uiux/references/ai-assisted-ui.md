# AI-assisted interface quality and homogenisation

Use this reference when reviewing interfaces produced with coding agents, or when output looks generic and you need to work out whether that is a real quality problem.

## The core stance

**Do not build or apply a visual "AI detector."** No sufficiently validated visual-provenance classifier exists in the reviewed evidence. Human designers use gradients, Inter, Tailwind, shadcn/ui, cards, and dark mode constantly; models also produce genuinely unusual layouts when well constrained.

Inferring AI authorship from visual appearance is unfounded and corrosive — it penalises ordinary human choices and excuses real defects behind a label.

The useful question is not *"was this made by AI?"* It is:

> **Does this interface show evidence of deliberate product design and complete engineering review?**

This reference therefore measures **generative-default risk**: how much of the interface was left to a default rather than a decision. A high score is not an accusation of authorship.

## Consistency vs homogenisation

The most useful distinction in this area:

- **Consistency** is purposeful reuse of patterns *within* one product.
- **Homogenisation** is convergence toward the same generic solution *across unrelated products*.

Peer-reviewed work (ACM DIS '25) evaluated 90 interfaces generated by two AI tools across three application domains, alongside interviews with eight professional designers, and found generated designs could achieve basic accessibility while relying on homogenised patterns that constrained creative flexibility and specialist user needs.

The cure is not random novelty or an eccentric layout. It is **more product-specific input**: real workflows, brand attributes, actual data, constraints, edge cases, and explicit rationale. Novelty-by-association produces a different generic template, not a better product.

## High-confidence quality signals

These are well-supported failure modes. They indicate incomplete review, regardless of who or what wrote the code.

### A. Only the happy path was designed

Symptoms: no loading, error, empty, offline, or permission states; buttons re-submittable during async work; inconsistent destructive-action protection; tables with no zero-results or overflow behaviour; forms with no validation strategy; dialogs with no focus-return plan.

Screenshot-oriented generation optimises the visible default state. Real products spend most of their design effort on states invisible in a showcase image.

### B. Superficial or absent accessibility semantics

Symptoms: clickable `div`/`span` instead of native controls; `<a>` used for actions; icon-only buttons without accessible names; inputs without persistent labels; heading hierarchy based on visual size rather than document structure; focus indicator removed or obscured; `role="dialog"` without keyboard/focus behaviour; status toasts invisible to assistive technology.

These map directly onto the peer-reviewed findings that generated code frequently needs accessibility remediation, and onto W3C guidance on native semantics. See [standards-targets.md](standards-targets.md).

### C. Structural sameness across unrelated pages

Symptoms: every page opens with large title + subtitle + right-aligned button; every section becomes the same three-column card grid; every dashboard module gets equal visual weight; every feature is icon + title + two-line description regardless of content; page types that should differ (browse vs edit vs analyse vs settings) share one generic composition.

This is the most direct visual implication of documented homogenisation.

### D. Content generic enough to fit any product

Symptoms: "Streamline your workflow", "Unlock insights", "Everything you need"; invented round-number metrics with no source; repeated placeholder companies; vague CTAs like "Get started" where a domain action would be clearer; empty charts that exist to make a dashboard look busy; terminology that shifts between pages.

Never invent trust signals, customer counts, awards, testimonials, or operational metrics to make generated UI look finished. Fabricated evidence is a more serious defect than bland copy — it can mislead real decisions.

### E. No coherent token logic

Symptoms: subtly different greys, radii, and gaps per page; arbitrary utility values accumulating without semantic roles; hard-coded colours inside components; dark mode overridden per component; 6–10 shadow variants with no elevation model; near-duplicate button variants.

An accessible, coherent design system is the constraint surface that prevents this. If the agent may invent values screen by screen, drift and defaults are the predictable outcome.

### F. Accidental responsive behaviour

Symptoms: desktop grid simply stacks at one breakpoint; action bars wrap unpredictably; mobile navigation generated separately and functionally divergent; long content overflows; fixed heights clip localised or user-scaled text; sticky headers obscure focus; modal content exceeds the viewport.

Agent-generated layouts often work at exact framework breakpoints with demo content and fail between them. Test 320/360/390/768/1024/1280+, zoom 200–400%, long and short content, and the mobile virtual keyboard.

## Medium-confidence generative defaults

Community-observed patterns, not authorship evidence. Treat them as prompts for review **when they appear together without a product-specific reason**:

- centred marketing hero with eyebrow badge, huge headline, two CTAs;
- exactly three equal feature cards beneath the hero;
- the same rounded rectangle, 1 px border, and soft shadow on nearly every region;
- default component-library visual language shipped with minimal adaptation;
- excessive rounded/pill shapes regardless of component role;
- purple/indigo-to-blue gradient standing in for brand identity;
- glow or aurora blobs behind otherwise ordinary content;
- glassmorphism on navigation or cards with no functional benefit;
- all type set in the safest common sans with little hierarchy;
- identical hover elevation or scale on every card;
- a saturated accent bar on the leading edge of every selected nav row, stacked on top of a filled card;
- reflexive dark mode used to signal sophistication;
- bento grids where content has no unequal importance;
- large whitespace around little content to simulate "premium".

Any one of these can be excellent design. The smell is **unmotivated combination and repetition**, not membership in a list.

## Signals that must never be used as accusations

Do not infer AI authorship from: a common font (Inter, Geist, Roboto); Tailwind CSS; shadcn/ui; Material or Fluent; rounded cards; a purple or blue colour; gradients; dark mode; Lucide or Heroicons; a bento grid; a component-library look.

The review target is lack of intent, not style membership.

## Specifying design instead of adjectives

Prompts like "make it modern, clean, premium, sleek" are under-specified. They give the agent no test to satisfy, so it must fill dozens of design variables from common patterns — which is precisely how generic output arises.

Specify instead: primary users and top tasks; the actual content on this screen; desired information density; hierarchy and focal priority; design tokens and permitted primitives; platform conventions; responsive behaviour; the state matrix; accessibility requirements; motion policy; performance budget; acceptance tests.

Research on professional "vibe coding" frames this as intent, context, and quality criteria rather than implementation preference. The agent should implement inside a design envelope, not invent the envelope from vague adjectives.

## The state matrix

Require this **before** implementation. If a state cannot be described, it should not be quietly invented in code.

| Component/flow | Default | Pending | Success | Empty | Validation error | System error | Permission | Offline | Focus/keyboard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Search | Yes | Yes | Yes | No results | N/A | API failed | N/A | Cached/retry | Full |
| Edit form | Yes | Submit pending | Saved | N/A | Field + summary | Save failed | Read-only | Draft retained | Full |
| Data table | Yes | Loading | Populated | No records | N/A | Load failed | Hidden/read-only | Cached | Row actions |

## De-genericisation review pass

Run after functional implementation. The goal is to find unexamined defaults, **not** to make things prettier:

- Does the screen look like this product's domain, or like a generic admin template?
- Could another company's logo be substituted without changing layout or content?
- Do all sections use the same visual archetype?
- Are components styled from product tokens, or carrying library defaults?
- Is any decoration present only because it is currently fashionable?
- Does information density match the job to be done?
- Does the layout still hold with realistic content?

Fix sameness by making content hierarchy and product identity more specific — not by adding random asymmetry or swapping to a different trend.

## Generic-default risk rubric

Score each category 0, 1, or 2 — **0** deliberate and complete, **1** partially specified or inconsistent, **2** generic, default, or incomplete.

| Category | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product specificity | tied to real tasks/domain | some generic sections | could belong to almost any product |
| Information architecture | task-led, prioritised | mostly clear | template-led or equal-weight |
| Visual system | explicit tokens and hierarchy | some drift | library/default values dominate |
| Component states | all relevant states designed | important gaps | happy path only |
| Accessibility | semantics + keyboard + manual review | automated checks only | repeated structural failures |
| Responsive behaviour | behaviour specified and tested | breakpoint stacking | desktop squeezed or stacked |
| Content | specific, consistent, realistic | some placeholder wording | boilerplate, invented metrics, vague CTAs |
| Motion | purposeful with reduced-motion path | some stock motion | decorative everywhere, no preference support |
| Performance | measured against budget | basic optimisation | unmeasured, heavy decorative work |
| Craft review | visual/state/a11y QA performed | informal | accepted after first plausible render |

Interpretation: **0–4** low risk · **5–9** review needed, visible default assumptions · **10–14** high risk of generic or incomplete implementation · **15–20** do not ship; rebuild the design constraints before polishing further.

This rubric is a synthesis created for the source research, not a published standard. Treat it as a structured prompt for scrutiny, not a score with external authority.

## Evidence discipline

The empirical support here is uneven, and honesty about that is part of the guidance.

- Strongest support: homogenisation and structural convergence (ACM DIS '25), and the finding that generated code frequently needs accessibility remediation (Springer UAIS '25 benchmark; ACM W4A '24; ACM ASSETS '24 on coding assistants).
- **Weakest support:** the quantitative error counts from one ASSETS '25 item. That work is an *extended abstract*, not a full-length peer-reviewed paper, and its headline figures were not independently confirmable from primary sources during this skill's verification pass. Do not restate those counts as established findings. The qualitative conclusion — that generated output requires human accessibility review — is independently corroborated by the other, better-verified studies and is safe to rely on.
- Tier D practitioner sources are observational only. They may corroborate an implementation smell already supported by stronger evidence. They never justify a hard requirement on their own, and they never establish authorship.

When product analytics, usability testing, accessibility requirements, or platform conventions contradict a heuristic here, the task-specific evidence wins.
